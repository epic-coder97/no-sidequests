# Learning-Science Basis

This resource explains the research principles behind the skill. It is not a script to reproduce for learners.

## 1. Cognitive load and coherence

Working memory is limited. Instruction becomes harder when the learner must process information that does not contribute to the current learning goal.

Design implication:
- remove decorative facts and redundant explanation;
- avoid visually competing elements;
- keep the immediate task small enough to mentally manipulate;
- do not confuse completeness with instructional usefulness.

## 2. Segmenting and learner pacing

Complex material is easier to process when broken into meaningful units rather than delivered as one continuous exposition.

Design implication:
- teach one mechanism or distinction at a time;
- stop at natural conceptual boundaries;
- let later detail build on an already-formed mental model.

## 3. Signaling

Learners benefit when presentation makes relevant structure and important information easier to identify.

Design implication:
- headings should communicate conceptual function;
- bold should signal genuinely important terms;
- diagrams should highlight relationships;
- formatting should guide attention rather than decorate the page.

## 4. Prior knowledge and analogy

New concepts are often learned by relating them to existing mental structures. Analogies can help when they preserve relevant relational structure, but they can also create misconceptions when surface similarity is mistaken for equivalence.

Design implication:
- explicitly map analogy elements to concept elements;
- state at least one important boundary when the analogy could overgeneralize;
- return to literal language after the analogy.

## 5. Retrieval practice

Trying to retrieve an idea from memory can strengthen later access more than passive rereading alone.

Design implication:
- ask learners to predict, reconstruct, compare, or explain;
- use errors as diagnostic evidence;
- keep retrieval prompts tightly aligned with the concept just taught.

## 6. Worked examples and fading

For novices learning procedures or problem-solving, worked examples can reduce unnecessary search and make solution structure visible. Support can then be faded as competence increases.

Design implication:
- begin with a clean representative example;
- explain important decisions, not every trivial operation;
- move to partially completed examples before fully independent work.

## 7. Contrast and misconception repair

Learning often improves when the boundary between similar cases is visible.

Design implication:
- use one near-miss or contrast case when classification boundaries matter;
- repair the specific broken relationship rather than repeating the whole lesson.

## 8. Multimedia principle, used cautiously

Visuals help when they represent structure that would otherwise be harder to infer. Extra visuals can also increase load.

Design implication:
- prefer explanatory visuals over decorative visuals;
- one diagram should answer one question;
- accompany a visual with a brief cue about what to notice.

## Maintenance note

When this skill is revised, prefer systematic reviews, meta-analyses, authoritative syntheses, and high-quality primary studies over isolated findings. Update behavioral rules only when evidence is strong enough to justify changing the teaching pattern.
