# Source Selection for Research-Sensitive Teaching

Use this resource when a lesson depends on current or contested evidence.

## Evidence hierarchy

Prefer, when relevant:

1. official standards, laws, regulations, or primary institutional data;
2. systematic reviews and meta-analyses;
3. high-quality primary research;
4. authoritative scientific or professional organizations;
5. strong secondary explainers for context;
6. community discussion only when lived experience or public reaction is itself relevant.

Do not treat this as a rigid ranking. A primary legal text is better than a review article for what a law says; a meta-analysis is often better than one experiment for estimating an educational effect.

## Freshness

Ask whether the fact can change.

Current verification is important for:
- laws and regulations,
- clinical or safety guidance,
- software and technical standards,
- statistics and economic indicators,
- active scientific debates,
- named officeholders or organizations,
- newly released research.

For stable foundational concepts, prioritize explanatory quality over publication recency.

## Evidence labels

When useful, distinguish:

**Well established** — supported across strong evidence or long-standing consensus.

**Current evidence** — supported by recent evidence but still subject to refinement.

**Uncertain / debated** — meaningful disagreement, limited data, or unstable estimates.

Do not turn every lesson into an epistemology lecture. Use these labels only when they help the learner calibrate confidence.

## Conflicting evidence

When credible sources disagree:

1. identify what exactly differs: population, method, outcome, timeframe, or interpretation;
2. explain the strongest common ground first;
3. state the disagreement compactly;
4. avoid presenting uncertainty as a 50/50 split unless it really is one.

## Citation design

Citations should support trust without breaking reading flow.

Prefer:
- citations at the end of the sentence or paragraph they support;
- 1–3 strong sources over a long bibliography in the teaching flow;
- a separate source note only if the learner asks for deeper research.

Avoid:
- citation clusters after every simple sentence;
- source names inserted into the middle of an explanation unless authorship itself matters;
- using low-quality sources merely because they are easier to explain.

## Research-to-teaching translation

Do not copy the structure of a paper into the lesson.

Translate research into:

`claim -> mechanism or interpretation -> concrete implication -> uncertainty if material`

The learner usually needs the model before the methodology. Provide study design details when they affect how much confidence to place in the conclusion.
